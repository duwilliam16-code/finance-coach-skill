# Investing basics - plain language

Use this when the person doesn't yet know the vocabulary. Explain with their
own accounts where you can; the examples here use a made-up person, Sam.

## The one idea most people miss

**Putting money into an account and investing it are two separate steps.**

Sam opened a Roth IRA and moved $3,000 into it. A year later it was still
$3,000. Why? The money arrived as **cash** and sat there. Sam never told the
account to *buy* anything. Always check: is the money invested, or is it cash?

## Account types (the containers)

An account is a container. What's *inside* it (cash, funds, stocks) is a
separate question.

| Container | Tax treatment in plain words |
|-----------|------------------------------|
| **401(k) / 403(b) / TSP** | Workplace retirement plan. Often comes with an employer **match** - free money for contributing |
| **Traditional (pre-tax)** | No tax on the money going in; taxed when you take it out in retirement |
| **Roth** | Taxed going in; grows and comes out **tax-free** in retirement |
| **IRA** | A retirement account you open yourself, outside work. Can be Traditional or Roth |
| **Brokerage (taxable)** | No special tax treatment, no limits, take money out any time |
| **HSA** | For people with a high-deductible health plan. Can be tax-free going in, growing and coming out for medical costs |

Yearly contribution limits apply to most of these. **Look them up each year
at irs.gov** - they change.

## What goes inside (the investments)

- **Stock** - a small piece of one company. Risky alone.
- **Bond** - a loan to a government or company. Steadier, grows less.
- **Fund** - a basket of many stocks or bonds bought in one go.
- **Index fund** - a fund that simply buys a whole market (e.g. all US
  companies). Cheap, diversified, and over long periods it beats most
  professionally picked funds.
- **ETF vs mutual fund** - two ways to package a fund. An ETF trades during
  the day like a stock. A mutual fund trades once a day at the close.

## Fees matter more than people think

The **expense ratio** is the yearly fee a fund charges, as a percentage.
Sam's $10,000 in a 1.00% fund costs $100 every year; in a 0.05% fund, $5.
Over decades that gap compounds into thousands of dollars.

## Simple beats clever

- **One broad, low-cost fund** (a total-world or target-date fund) is a
  complete portfolio for most people. Owning ten overlapping funds isn't more
  diversified - often it's the same companies several times over.
- **Time in the market beats timing the market.** Invest steadily; don't wait
  for the "right moment".
- **Match risk to the timeline.** Money needed within a few years
  (emergency fund, house down payment) belongs in savings, not stocks.
